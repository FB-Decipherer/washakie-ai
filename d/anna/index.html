A  Plain answers
What am I looking at?

A page built the way yours would be built. One topic, one page, and every claim on it carries the source it came from — the column in the middle.

It loads in under a second on any telephone, including out at a job site. It has no analytics, no trackers and no cookies. Nothing on it is watching you.

Tap whichever fits you. Both companies are invented and say so across the top. Everything else on them — the insurance rules, the safety standard, the county’s crops and water, the State of Wyoming’s own verification — is real and sourced. Both are previewed in the middle column: the tree service1 and the farm5.

A trade — tree service →Insurance first, because that is what a stranger most wants to know and least likes asking. Tap the verification button yourself; that is the part worth seeing.

A farm — hay and beef →What is for sale, what hay costs, how buying a quarter of beef actually works, and why you cannot just buy a steak.

What would mine look like?

The same shape, with your answers in it. The questions a customer actually asks you, answered plainly, in the order they ask them.

For a trade, the first answer is usually insurance, because that is what a stranger most wants to know and least likes asking. For a shop it is hours and what you carry. For a farm it is what is for sale and when.

You would not write it. You would talk, and someone would write it down and show it to you until it was right.

What does it cost?

Nothing, to have one. The hosting is free and stays free — there is no subscription, no platform fee and no bill of any kind2. The address would look like washakieai.github.io/yourfarm.

About a dollar a month, if you want your own name on it — roughly twelve to fifteen dollars a year for something like eastbenchfarm.com, paid to whoever sells you the name and to nobody else.

That second one is worth saying plainly, because it is not only about looks. The name is the part you actually own. With your own name you can pick the whole thing up and move it anywhere; on the free address you cannot, because the address belongs to the company hosting it4.

So: free to find out whether you want one. A dollar a month when you decide you do.

For the pilot program the building is free as well3. If it goes ahead, the first businesses pay nothing at all.

Do I own it?

The name and the words are yours outright. That is the honest answer, and it is unusual enough to be worth saying plainly.

The page is a handful of plain files. If you ever want to move it, you take the files and the address and go — there is nothing to export and nobody to ask. On a social page you cannot do that: the address was never yours and the audience does not come with you4.

Why is a high school student asking me this?

Because nobody has built one of these for a Worland business yet, and before anyone asks the county to pay for twelve of them, somebody ought to find out whether businesses here actually want one.

That is what today is. I show you the example, you tell me what you think of it, and I write down what you said. It goes on a list that decides whether the program happens 3.

There is nothing to sign and nothing to buy. If you think it is useless, that is worth knowing too, and I will write that down just as carefully.

What happens to what I tell you?

It stays in this telephone until the end of the day, and then it goes to Walter Wilkinson in Worland, who is running this. It is not uploaded anywhere as we speak and nobody else sees it.

You decide whether your name is on it. I will ask you outright. If you would rather not be named, the list says “a business on Big Horn Avenue” and your answers still count.

C  How it works (Details)

Prior know-how is assumed here, and the items become more technical toward the bottom. This column is the student’s working side: the call is written down here, once the conversation is over.

The call

Fill this in after you have shown them the example, not during. Everything stays in this telephone until you send it.

Show the one that fits. The farm page for anyone selling hay, beef, grain or ground; the tree service for a trade, a contractor or anyone whose customers worry about insurance. Showing a rancher a tree service page makes them do the translating, and some of them will not bother.

1. Do they have a website now?
2. Having seen it — would they want a page like that?
3. What would they most want on it?
4. Would they talk to someone about building one?
5. May we name them on a list of interested businesses?

Ask it out loud. If they say no, their answers still count.

    Nothing saved yet.

    Where this lives. Every answer is held in this telephone’s own memory and nowhere else until you tap send. There is no server behind this page — that is true of the whole format, and it is why the call sheet had to be built this way rather than as a form that posts somewhere. Send at the end of each day: if the telephone is wiped first, they are gone.

    Working with no signal

    The page registers a small service worker that keeps a copy of itself, the icons and the portrait in the browser’s cache. Once it has been opened with a signal, it opens again without one — in a shop with thick walls, in a shed, out past Ten Sleep. Every fetch also refreshes the cache quietly in the background, so a republished page is picked up next time rather than being stuck forever behind a stale copy.

    Deliberately, the page loads no web fonts. The rest of this family uses IBM Plex Mono and Source Serif; this instance uses whatever the telephone already has. A typeface that arrives over the network is one more thing that fails at the moment it is needed.

    The icon with her name on it

    Opened in a browser and added to the home screen, this page gets an icon of its own carrying the student’s name, and opens full-screen with no address bar. That is a web app manifest — about forty lines — and not an App Store application: no developer account, no review, no annual fee. Functionally it is theirs; it simply was not downloaded from Apple.

    The icon artwork keeps everything inside the middle eighty per cent of the square, because Android crops a maskable icon to a circle and would otherwise take the name off the bottom.

    The paper fallback

    Printed, this column becomes the same five questions blank, with ruled lines to write on and every button, tally and saved call left off the page. A telephone runs out of battery. Paper does not.

    Why this is an instance and not a form

    The first version of this page was a plain input form in the format’s colors. It worked, and it demonstrated nothing: a student holding it out was showing a questionnaire, not a website. Walter caught it in one line — “I had in mind an instance of 3Col.”

    Built this way the page argues for itself. The owner reads column A while the student holds the telephone; the sources are one tap away in column B if they want to check anything; and the working machinery sits in column C where a customer has no reason to wander. The demonstration and the tool are the same object.

    Failures recorded in building this

    Per the rule in column A, and these are real:

    • It was built as a form first. Recorded above. The mistake was building the thing that collects the answer instead of the thing that earns it.
    • The print layout printed the same heading twice and drew a solid black block where ruled lines were meant to be — the lines were faked with a box-shadow instead of being real elements. Fixed by making them real.
    • Footnote markers were put inside the example cards, where the card is itself a link. Tapping the numeral followed the link instead of opening the source, because a control inside a control belongs to the outer one. Caught by testing the tap rather than by looking at it. The markers now sit in the sentence above the cards.
    • The first icon had the name cropped off under Android’s circular mask, because the artwork used the whole square instead of the safe centre. Caught by rendering the mask and looking, not by reading the specification.
